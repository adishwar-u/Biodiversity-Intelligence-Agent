---
title: "Soil Organic Carbon: the Hidden Potential"
org: FAO
year: 2017
topic: soil
---

Soil organic carbon (SOC) is the single largest terrestrial carbon pool and the
strongest single indicator of soil health. Depleted SOC reduces aggregate
stability, water-holding capacity and microbial activity, creating a
self-reinforcing degradation cycle: lower SOC means less water retained after
rain, which means less plant biomass returned to the soil, which further
lowers SOC.

Practices that add organic inputs — legume cover cropping, composted manure,
residue retention — reliably raise SOC over a 2-5 year horizon in tropical and
sub-tropical cropland, though the rate depends heavily on baseline SOC, texture
and rainfall. Sandy, low-rainfall soils respond more slowly than clay-rich,
higher-rainfall soils to the same input regime.

Each 1% increase in SOC is associated with roughly a 1.5-2% increase in
available water-holding capacity by volume, because organic matter both binds
soil particles into stable aggregates (increasing pore space) and directly
absorbs water. This is the mechanical link between carbon-building practices
and drought resilience: SOC does not just store carbon, it changes how much
of each rainfall event the soil can keep.
