Land degradation and desertification are driven overwhelmingly by human
land-use decisions — overgrazing, deforestation, and continuous
monoculture without organic inputs — rather than by climate alone, though
climate variability accelerates degradation once it begins. Exposed,
structurally simplified soil erodes faster under both wind and water,
and the resulting loss of the organic-rich topsoil layer removes the same
water-holding capacity that would otherwise buffer the land against the
drought conditions that further accelerate degradation.

Reversing this trajectory generally requires re-establishing vegetation
cover — whether tree, shrub or grass — because cover is the mechanism that
breaks the erosion cycle: it intercepts rainfall energy, slows wind speed
at the soil surface, and returns organic matter to the topsoil through
litter and root turnover. Windbreaks and shelterbelts specifically target
wind-driven soil loss, reducing wind speed for a distance of several times
the barrier's height on the leeward side, and are among the fastest
interventions to show a measurable erosion reduction, typically within two
to three years of establishment.
