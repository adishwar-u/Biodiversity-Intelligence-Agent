---
title: "Landscape Corridors and Habitat Connectivity"
org: IUCN
year: 2016
topic: biodiversity
---

Habitat fragmentation reduces biodiversity through a mechanism distinct
from habitat loss itself: even when total habitat area is held constant,
splitting it into smaller, disconnected patches isolates populations,
increases edge effects and raises local extinction risk for species that
need to move between patches to feed, breed or disperse genetically.

Linear semi-natural features — hedgerows, field margins, riparian buffers,
beetle banks — function as movement corridors that reconnect otherwise
isolated patches. Even narrow corridors measurably increase the exchange
of individuals between patches, and fields bordered by such margins show
a larger and more stable pool of resident plant and insect species than
margin-free fields of the same size.

These same margins double as overwintering refuge for predatory and
parasitoid arthropods, since undisturbed tussocky vegetation survives the
tillage and harvest cycles that reset the cropped area each season. This is
why margin restoration shows up as both a biodiversity and a natural
pest-control intervention in the literature.
