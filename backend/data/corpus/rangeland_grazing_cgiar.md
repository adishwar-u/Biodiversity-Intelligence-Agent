---
title: "Rangeland Management and Livestock Productivity"
org: CGIAR
year: 2018
topic: land_use
---

Continuous grazing — livestock left on the same land indefinitely — keeps
vegetation cropped short, preventing the root-reserve recovery and canopy
regrowth that build soil organic matter over time. Rotational grazing,
where paddocks are rested in sequence, allows vegetation to complete a
recovery cycle before being grazed again, and this recovery is what drives
the downstream soil benefit: more root biomass returned to the soil, and
more standing litter, each grazing cycle.

Long-term rotational grazing trials show a slow but real rise in soil
organic carbon (roughly 0.05-0.15% absolute over five to eight years),
alongside a shift toward greater sward height variation across the
paddock rotation — some paddocks resting and tall, others freshly grazed
and short — which adds structural diversity that continuously-grazed,
uniformly-short pasture lacks.

The practical barrier to adoption is infrastructure: subdividing land into
rotational paddocks requires fencing and, in drier rangelands, additional
water points so livestock are not forced back to a single source that
undoes the rest-period benefit.
